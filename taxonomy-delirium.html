Illustrative application

Three delirium prediction models, read through the taxonomy

To examine whether the dimensions can be recognized in concrete prediction model reports, we applied the taxonomy informally to three delirium prediction studies listed in the supplementary material of a recent scoping review of machine learning for delirium prediction using electronic health records. The exercise was intended to demonstrate interpretability of the taxonomy, not to estimate the frequency of communication problems in delirium prediction.

Jauk et al.

JAMIA · 2020

Random forest model implemented in an Austrian hospital network and evaluated prospectively over seven months.

On the clinician-facing display

  • A color-coded icon in the patient list
  • Red for the very high risk group (95th to 100th percentile), yellow for the high risk group (85th to 94th percentile)
  • No symbol for patients classified as low risk

Reported in the manuscript

  • AUROC prospective 0.855
  • AUROC test 0.854
  • Calibration deteriorated; predicted risks were systematically higher than observed event rates
  • No management recommendation for patients identified as high risk

The reliability limitation was measured and reported in the manuscript but was not conveyed through the clinician-facing display. The study also illustrates the distinction between risk identification and decision support.

doi:10.1093/jamia/ocaa113 ↗

Lee et al.

BMC Psychiatry · 2023

Gradient boosting model for delirium after noncardiac surgery, released as a web application.

On the clinician-facing display

  • A predicted probability, returned from five input variables

Reported in the manuscript

  • Threshold 0.020
  • Sensitivity 0.76
  • Specificity 0.84
  • External event rate 0.3%
  • AUPRC 0.062
  • F1 0.064
  • The low base rate and precision-recall performance are reported in the paper but are not apparent from the probability the tool returns

A clinician receives an individualized estimate without information about the low base rate, precision-recall performance, or how those characteristics should shape interpretation. Making a model accessible is not equivalent to making the reliability of its output interpretable.

doi:10.1186/s12888-023-04768-y ↗

Benovic et al.

Age and Ageing · 2024

Delirium prediction component for a geriatric co-management system (SURGE-Ahead), with clinician-facing communication requirements as explicit design goals.

On the clinician-facing display

  • A calibrated probability estimate, accompanied by the features contributing most to the prediction
  • Expressed as a probability rather than a categorical directive, preserving space for clinical interpretation

Reported in the manuscript

  • Internal-external cross-validation across centers
  • Potential algorithmic bias assessed by sex and native language with the Aequitas framework; ethnicity data noted as unavailable
  • Published linear decision function, coefficients, normalization values, and the mean/median values used for missing inputs

The study does not address every dimension of the taxonomy, but it illustrates that several communication dimensions can be addressed within the development and reporting of a clinical prediction model.

doi:10.1093/ageing/afae101 ↗

These three cases are illustrative rather than systematic. They were not sampled for comparative evaluation, no formal coding instrument or inter-rater procedure was applied, and no claim is made about the frequency of any communication pattern in the broader delirium literature. They show that the dimensions can be applied to published prediction model reports and that they differentiate among communication approaches rather than labeling all studies identically.
